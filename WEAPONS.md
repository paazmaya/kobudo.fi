# Various weapons use in Ryukyu martial arts

## Reference backbone from Japanese libraries

Two key Japanese reference works underpin much of the good web material:

- **『沖縄空手古武道事典』高宮城繁・新里勝彦・仲本政博 編著（柏書房, 2008）** – a 752‑page encyclopedia with a full "古武道編" including chapters on _代表的武器術_ (representative weapons), _代表的各武器術の分解_ (detailed breakdowns for each weapon), and "沖縄伝統古武道の型". [kashiwashobo.co](https://www.kashiwashobo.co.jp/book/9784760133697) [kinokuniya.co.jp](https://www.kinokuniya.co.jp/f/dsg-01-9784760133697) ⚠️ **Publisher site currently inaccessible**
- **『沖縄伝統古武道 初級 その型と応用』仲本政博 著（文武館, 1989）** – its table of contents (archived by the Okinawa Prefectural Archives) shows sections "概略 武器の紹介 古武術 … 型と応用", presenting both historical overviews and weapon introductions before kata and applications. [www2.archives.pref.okinawa](https://www2.archives.pref.okinawa.jp/opa/OPA600_RESULT_BUNSYO.aspx?cont_cd=T00018093B&lang=jp)

Many Japanese web articles explicitly cite one or both of these as their sources. [odks](https://www.odks.jp/kobudo/history.html) ⚠️ **Model page inaccessible; history page available**

## Bō (棒術・棍法)

- A long article “琉球古武道武器術” by 揆奮館流武術 explains that **bōjutsu was originally the weapon of government officials (role similar to modern police)** in the Ryukyu Kingdom, used for controlling disturbances rather than battlefield mass combat. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm) ⚠️ **Link currently inaccessible**
- The same essay notes that Chinese military treatises such as **『武備志』 and 『紀効新書』** contain staff methods whose technical content closely resembles modern Okinawan bōjutsu, and quotes "all martial arts take staff (棍) as their root," emphasizing that staff work is the foundation of weapons practice. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm) ⚠️ **Link currently inaccessible**
- It distinguishes Chinese staff (slender, whippy, skin‑cutting) from Okinawan staff (shorter, thicker, bone‑breaking) and describes traditional Ryukyu staff wood as **kuba** (クバ, a tough wood with wavy grain), before modern red/white oak became common; it notes that kingdom‑era guard bō were often five shaku, not the modern standard of six shaku. [okinawatimes.co](https://www.okinawatimes.co.jp/articles/-/821696)
- According to Japanese Wikipedia, Okinawan bō typically uses **six-shaku staff** (approximately 181.80cm), with ends that are slightly thinner than the center. The shape reflects its origin as a carrying pole (担ぎ棒) for agricultural use, making it distinct from other regional staff traditions. Many kata are named after famous samurai or use Chinese-derived names. [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/琉球古武術)

## Sai (釵術)

- The same 揆奮館 article describes sai as a **capture weapon** used by officials (役人) to arrest and control people, functionally similar to Japan's jitte but typically used in pairs or trios in Okinawa. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- It states that sai technique is built around both **defense and offense**: used to receive and trap swords, poles, or other weapons, then strike or lock joints; throwing sai is also mentioned as part of the classical repertoire. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- Several modern dojo pages and seminars by Okinawan institutions present sai as a **non‑bladed metal weapon** suited to the Ryukyuan legal environment (where swords were restricted), reinforcing this “law‑enforcement tool” origin rather than an agricultural one. [www8.cao.go](https://www8.cao.go.jp/okinawa/okinawakarate/shokai/karate-shokai.html)
- According to Ryukyu Kobudo sources, the character for sai (釵) means "hairpin" but the weapon may have originated from Indian Buddhist implements shaped like the human body. In the Ryukyu Kingdom era, officials called _ufuchiku_ (大筑, similar to modern police chiefs) carried sai as symbols of rank and for self-defense, crowd control, and guidance duties. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/) [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/琉球古武術)

## Tonfa / Tunkuwa (トンファー／トンクワァ)

- 揆奮館流's essay identifies **柱拐 (tunkuwa / tonfa)** as a weapon originating in **Fuzhou, China**, where the weapon and its techniques were known and the term _tonkua_ is used in the local dialect. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- It explains that tonfa developed from the **handle of a mortar (臼の柄ヘラ)** and was primarily a **defensive weapon**: held in both hands to receive attacks from swords, spears, or bō, with enough shock power that an opponent's weapon could be knocked from the hand. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- The Ryukyu Kobudo weapons page describes modern tonfa as a **"T‑shaped short stick with a side handle"**, emphasizing its versatility for striking, blocking, and hooking, and notes that because of its practicality, tonfa derivatives have been formally adopted by police forces worldwide as side‑handle batons. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- Alternative origin theories include the stone mill handle (石臼の挽き棒) and agricultural farming tools (ヘラ). Tonfa requires some training compared to bō or nunchaku but offers rich variations in spinning, thrusting, and sweeping motions for both offense and defense. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/) [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/琉球古武術)

## Nunchaku (ヌンチャク)

- 揆奮館の記事 gives a vivid origin story: after the 1609 Satsuma invasion, when weapons were restricted, **women are said to have devised nunchaku from a tool used to strip banana fiber (クーラ)**, turning it into a self‑defense weapon; nunchaku thus evolved as a **concealed personal protection tool**, not a battlefield weapon. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- Another Japanese explanation on the Ryukyu Kobudo weapons page notes that nunchaku, like other **暗器 (hidden weapons)**, was **originally a hunting tool**; with its short reach and need for precise control, it emphasizes receiving first, which is why nunchaku kata traditionally **begin with receiving techniques rather than direct attacks**. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- Okinawan dojo and federation sites commonly emphasize nunchaku's role as a later addition to kobudō, popularized for demonstration and training but historically secondary compared to staff and spear‑like weapons. [okinawabbtv](https://www.okinawabbtv.com/desc/%E7%A9%BA%E6%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E6%AD%B4%E5%8F%B22-%E6%B2%96%E7%B8%84%E3%81%AE%E4%BC%9D%E7%B5%B1%E5%8F%A4%E6%AD%A6%E9%81%93/)
- According to Ryukyu Kobudo sources, nunchaku (also called ヌウチク in some schools) may have originated from horse bridles (ムーゲー). Its purpose is to weaken and neutralize unreasonable violence or multiple attackers rather than aggressive use, which is why kata traditionally begin with receiving techniques. It was also used as a hunting tool originally. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/)

## Kama / Nichō‑gama (鎌術・二丁鎌)

- 揆奮館’s section on **鎌術（鎌之手）** dates its emergence to around 700 years ago, in **King Eiso’s era**, describing kama as an evolution from the agricultural **kuwa (hoe)** in the farmer’s repertoire and later refined through contact with Chinese martial arts. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- The article notes that Ryukyu kama practice includes:
  - **Double kama** (one in each hand).
  - **Rope‑attached kama** (鎌に紐を付ける).
  - **Long‑handled kama** mounted on a five‑shaku staff for extended reach.
- The Ryukyu Kobudo introduction page of another organization calls **Kanegawa no Nichō‑gama** a representative double‑kama kata, illustrating how kama was formalized into named forms and linked to specific locales and families. [jrkf.clouver](https://jrkf.clouver.jp/kobudo.html)
- According to Ryukyu Kobudo sources, kama are agricultural sickles used directly as weapons without modification. Since they are bladed weapons, they are practical but dangerous in practice, and high-level practitioners typically teach them. The "Kanegawa no Nichō-gama" kata is a representative double-kama form passed down in specific lineages. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/)

## Tekko and Tetchū (鉄甲・鉄柱)

- The Ryukyu Kobudo weapons page explains that **tekko** originally came from **horse shoes (馬蹄)** used in agriculture: people realized that if the iron shoe was held in the hand, it could reinforce the fist so that existing karate punches could be used almost unchanged, making tekko a very efficient weapon to learn and employ. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- It notes that traditional tekko had **protruding spikes**, but modern practice usually uses rounded ends for safety. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- 揆奮館 also describes **鉄柱（寸鉄）**, a roughly 15‑cm iron rod with a ring, used both as a palm weapon and for throwing, likening it to small shuriken‑like projectiles; this shows that **throwable iron rods** were part of Okinawan weapons culture, not only chains or stones. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viQxoozR1fI)

## Tinbē–Rōchin (ティンベー・ローチン)

- JRKF’s “What is Ryukyu Kobudo” page, written in Japanese, explains that **tinbē (shield) and rōchin (short spear)** form a paired weapon set; shields were traditionally made from rattan, leather, or turtle shell, while rōchin could be a short spear or machete‑like blade, and both could be used for feints, blinding, cutting, and thrusting. [jrkf.clouver](https://jrkf.clouver.jp/kobudo.html)
- The Ryukyu Kobudo weapons page adds that modern tinbē may be made from light alloys or wood, but the tactical idea remains: **use the shield for protection, vision disruption, and edge strikes, while the rochin stabs and cuts**, echoing southern Chinese shield‑and‑spear systems. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- 揆奮館 traces tinbē techniques back to southern Shaolin (南派小林拳の流れをくむ) and asserts that they were in actual use by the **Three Kingdoms (三山) period**, making tinbē one of the oldest layers of Ryukyuan weapon practice. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- According to Ryukyu Kobudo sources, tinbē (shield) is paired with rochin (short spear). Traditional shields were made from bamboo, leather, or turtle shell, while modern versions may use light alloys or wood. The rochin can be a short spear or machete-like blade (青龍刀). This pairing reflects southern Chinese shield-and-spear martial traditions. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/)

## Suruchin (スルチン / スルチン術)

- The Ryukyu Kobudo weapons article defines **suruchin** as a flexible weapon made by tying **two fist‑sized stones** together with _shuro‑nawa_ (棕櫚縄, palm fiber rope), later evolving into various chain or rope‑and‑weight combinations. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- Its use is described as **striking and entangling**: swung to hit directly, or to wrap around an opponent’s limbs or weapons to control or disarm them. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons)
- 揆奮館 mentions suruchin in the context of China‑influenced flexible weapons (together with sansetsukon), stressing how these required high skill and bodily integration, using the whole body to generate force and control the weapon's path. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudukijutu.htm)
- According to Ryukyu Kobudo sources, suruchin (also called 棕櫚鎮) connects two striking weapons with rope or chain. The original form used fist-sized stones tied with palm fiber rope (棕櫚縄, shuro-nawa). The name "suru" comes from the palm (シュロ, shuro/suru) used for the rope. Like nunchaku, it was used as a hidden weapon (暗器) and hunting tool, swung to strike or entangle opponents' limbs or weapons. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/)

## Eku / Kai (櫂術・エーク)

- 揆奮館’s section on eku describes it as **“a fisherman’s blade comparable to a sword”**, emphasizing that the oar (櫂) was the **fisherman’s principal weapon**, used both for paddling and for striking and cutting in close combat. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- It notes that eku’s **edge, shaft, and water‑scooping end** all have tactical roles: the blade edge can cut, the shaft can strike, and the flat end can scoop sand or water into the opponent’s eyes (“砂掛け”), echoing the famous Tsuken Sunakake oar kata. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- Okinawan cultural and UNESCO‑related materials group eku with kama, tonfa, and kuwa as examples of **agricultural or fishing tools that became weapons**, reinforcing that eku grew directly from daily livelihood implements. [d3b](https://www.d3b.jp/npcolumn/8890)
- According to Ryukyu Kobudo sources, eku (also called ウェーク or エーク) is the oar used for propelling sabani boats. Fishermen used these directly as weapons. Kata include techniques for throwing sand into opponents' eyes (砂掛け), simulating combat on beaches. This represents another example of fishing tools adapted for martial use. [ryukyukobudo](https://www.ryukyukobudo.net/ryukyu_kobudo/kobudo_weapons/)

## Kuwa (鍬術・鍬之手)

- 揆奮館 explains that **鍬術（鍬之手）** developed in parallel with kama: farmers used the hoe both as a digging tool and as a defensive weapon, and over time, warriors refined its methods into a formal curriculum. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- It details how the hoe’s **blade, back, and handle** are all used for attack and defense, and notes that special techniques include flinging dug‑up earth into the opponent’s face to blind or disturb them, making **environmental manipulation** part of the weapon’s tactical identity. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- Later, with influence from Chinese spear and halberd methods, the movement vocabulary for hoe (kuwa) expanded into more sophisticated patterns, but it remained symbolically the **farmer's weapon** in Ryukyuan lore. [d3b](https://www.d3b.jp/npcolumn/8890)

## Nunti / Nuntisai / Nunti‑bō (貫手棒・ヌンティ棒／ヌンティ釵)

- A modern Japanese explanation video from 龍ケ崎光道館 describes **nunti‑bō (貫手棒)** as a traditional tool passed down in the Matayoshi family, with strong similarity to Chinese three‑prong spears called **虎叉 (ko‑sa)** known since the Three Kingdoms era. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e86AsmSyJiA)
- The same explanation notes that, according to Shinpo Matayoshi, **nunti‑bō has been transmitted since the early Ryukyu Kingdom**, and that **the original form combined a bamboo staff with a concealed nunti‑sai**: the metal nunti‑sai was hidden inside the staff and thrown at the opponent’s weapon, after which the remaining staff was used like a spear. [youtube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e86AsmSyJiA)
- 揆奮館’s essay ties nunti to **Chinese Wubeizhi’s 叉武** and describes the Ryukyuan version as a **five‑shaku spear with a manji‑sai head**, used both for thrusting and for sophisticated trapping and disarming, thus bridging spear‑ and sai‑based tactics. [kihunkan](http://kihunkan.net/ryukyukobudobukijutu.htm)
- ODks’s “Okinawa Traditional Kobudō” kata list includes **鐘川のティンベー（カニガーヌティンベー） and ヌンティー**, explicitly listing nunti as one of the “other weapons” and citing _Okinawa Karate Kobudo Jiten_ as its reference. [odks](https://www.odks.jp/kobudo/model.html)
- According to Japanese Wikipedia, nunti (ヌンティ) is a spear-like weapon with a manji-sai (卍釵) at the tip. It may have originated from fishing harpoons (もり). Nunti techniques are transmitted in the Kinkō-ryū lineage. This weapon bridges spear and sai tactics, combining thrusting with sophisticated trapping and disarming methods. [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/琉球古武術)

## How this ties back to Taira’s eight weapons

Taira’s eight‑weapon curriculum (bō, sai, tonfa, nunchaku, kama, tekko, tinbē–rōchin, suruchin) sits squarely on top of this older Ryukyuan weapons culture:

- **Bō, sai, tonfa, eku, kama, tekko, suruchin, tinbē, nunti, kuwa, tetchū** all have distinct historical narratives in Ryukyuan society as summarized above. [d3b](https://www.d3b.jp/npcolumn/8890)
- Taira and his students selected eight of these as a **teachable core**, then later generations (Matayoshi, Tokushinryū, Kongo‑ryū, Kenshin‑ryū, etc.) added or emphasized others—especially **nunti and kuwa**—while drawing on the same body of historical knowledge preserved and analyzed in works like _Okinawa Karate Kobudo Jiten_ and Nakamoto Masahiro’s _Okinawa Dento Kobudo_. [kinokuniya.co](https://www.kinokuniya.co.jp/f/dsg-01-9784760133697)
